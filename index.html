What is gravity?

Gravity is the attractive force between objects that have mass. It keeps planets moving around the Sun and gives objects weight on the surface of a planet.

Mass and weight are different

Mass stays the same wherever an object goes. Weight changes because each planet has a different surface gravitational acceleration.

W = m × g

Newton's law of universal gravitation

Every mass attracts every other mass. The force grows with the product of the masses and falls with the square of the distance between them. The values below are the Earth and the Moon.

Gravitational field

The field is the force a mass M produces on each kilogram placed at distance r. It points toward M. At Earth's surface it equals the acceleration due to gravity, about 9.81 m/s².

g = GM / r²

Gravitational potential

Potential is the work done per kilogram in bringing a body from infinity to a point. It is negative because gravity is attractive. Setting kinetic energy equal to this depth gives the escape velocity.

V = −GM / r

Kepler's laws

  • Planets move in ellipses with the Sun at one focus.
  • A line from the Sun to a planet sweeps equal areas in equal times.
  • The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the orbit's size: T² ∝ r³.

Beyond Newton: modern gravitation

Newton's law works very well for planets and satellites. Einstein's theory is needed when gravity is extremely strong or when precision matters.

General relativity

In 1915 Einstein described gravity as the curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy. Matter tells spacetime how to curve, and spacetime tells matter how to move. The theory explains the bending of starlight by the Sun and the slow rotation of Mercury's orbit, which Newton's law could not fully explain.

Gμν = (8πG / c⁴) Tμν

Black holes

Inside the event horizon, not even light can escape. The horizon's radius for a mass M is the Schwarzschild radius.

Gravitational waves

Accelerating masses, such as two black holes merging, send ripples through spacetime. LIGO made the first direct detection in September 2015, a century after Einstein predicted them. The 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded for this work. The first image of a black hole, in the galaxy M87, followed in 2019.
